Design notes — the calls made here, and how to overrule them
1. This map does not plot graves. That is the whole design.
Precise burial locations are withheld by archaeologists, descendant communities and states because publishing them invites desecration. In the United States site-location data is exempt from disclosure under NHPA §304 and ARPA §9 for exactly that reason, and NAGPRA notices themselves name only counties of removal, never coordinates.
So the unit of this map is the accountable actor and the decision, not the grave:
  • A repatriation notice sits at the institution holding the remains.
  • A heritage-harm permit sits at the centre of the permit area.
  • A review sits at the administrative unit it names.
  • Anything that is itself a burial location is blurred to about 5 km and drawn as a halo with no centre.
The blur is enforced by a gate keyed to the record type, not to the good intentions of whoever wrote the fetcher: hand it exact coordinates for a mass grave and it blurs them anyway. Unknown record types fail safe to blurred, and an audit pass re-checks every record before the file is written. If you disagree with this call, it is one constant to change — COARSE_GRID_DEG — and one table, KINDS, that decides which record types get blurred. 2. Spain is the case that tests the rule Spain's state map of the disappeared publishes grave coordinates deliberately, so families can find their dead. That looked like grounds for an exemption. But Ley 20/2022 art. 17 both mandates the map and requires the zones it names to receive preservación especial — Spain publishes and protects at once. So the blur stays even there. Separately, the Spanish data is not machine-readable anyway: the live tool is georeferenced, while the downloadable release dates from 2017 and carries no coordinates at all. 3. Two layers, two different rules, on purpose The cemetery layer is not blurred, and that is not an inconsistency:
  • A working cemetery is a signposted public place with a street address. People need to find it. Blurring it would serve nobody.
  • An archaeological or unmarked burial site is the opposite: unadvertised, and endangered by publication.
So the facility harvester takes only tags denoting an established public burial facility, and explicitly refuses historic=*, tombs, tumuli, necropoleis, and every lifecycle-prefixed tag (was:, demolished:, removed:, abandoned:). Those last are unearthing events and belong to the other harvester, where the blur applies. An element carrying both a cemetery tag and an archaeological tag is dropped rather than published. 4. Posture is not a quality score A repatriation and a permit to destroy are both on this map. Collapsing them into a single "impact" number would have made the map say something false, so posture carries direction (harm / watch / redress / unlawful) and scale carries magnitude only. A large repatriation is a large event, not a bad one. 5. Counts are never estimated A number of individuals appears only where a source states one, after an explicit "at least" / "a minimum of" / "a total of". Absence of a count means the source was silent — never that the number is small. 6. No coverage percentage is claimed No dataset holds the true global count of unearthings, so any percentage would be invented. Coverage is weighted toward jurisdictions with statutory repatriation and heritage-permit regimes, because those are the ones that publish. Sparse regions on this map are sparse in the records, not necessarily in the ground. The same caution applies to the wire: an empty wire for a region is a gap in reporting, not evidence that nothing is happening. 7. The lenses ship unverified, then get checked in public The resource lenses are hand-compiled, and curation rots faster than data. So they ship without any claim that every link works, and a scheduled link-checker verifies them. Dead links are greyed out and labelled, not deleted — a resource that has vanished tells you an office closed or a programme was wound up, which is itself worth knowing.
Where this data comes from & what’s not (yet) in it
What this map is Every mark is a public record of an unearthing or a decision about unearthed human remains — a repatriation notice, a permit to disturb a burial ground, an environmental review that admits graves are in the way, a burial ground recorded as removed. Records are harvested automatically from official registers and community databases, and each mark links back to its primary source. What this map deliberately will not do It does not publish grave coordinates. Precise burial locations are withheld by archaeologists, descendant communities and states because publishing them invites desecration — in the United States site-location data is exempt from disclosure under NHPA s.304 and ARPA s.9 for exactly that reason. So the unit of this map is the accountable actor and the decision, not the grave:
  • A repatriation notice sits at the institution holding the remains.
  • A heritage-harm permit sits at the centre of the permit area.
  • A review sits at the administrative unit it names.
  • Anything that is itself a burial location is blurred to about 5 km and drawn as a halo with no centre.
The blurring is enforced in the harvester by a fail-safe gate keyed to the record type, plus an audit pass before writing, so a source cannot publish a precise burial point by accident. Feeds in production
  • United States — NAGPRA notices. Notices of Inventory Completion, Intent to Repatriate, Intended Disposition, and Transfer, from the Federal Register API. This is the statutory venue: if remains are being repatriated from a US museum or agency, it is published here.
  • United States — federal reviews naming burials. NEPA and NHPA notices whose text puts human remains in a project’s path. Placed at state level only; these notices carry no coordinates.
  • Australia (NSW) — Aboriginal Heritage Impact Permits. Legal authorisations under s.90 of the National Parks and Wildlife Act 1974 to harm Aboriginal objects and places. Boundary polygons, quarterly refresh. NSW DCCEEW, CC-BY. DCCEEW notes some permits are missing from the spatial layer; the AHIP Public Register is authoritative.
  • California — CEQA filings. State clearinghouse filings naming burials or remains. California’s AB 52 tribal-consultation track means disclosure lands here earlier than in federal notices.
  • United Kingdom — planning applications. Local-authority applications affecting burial grounds, via PlanIt. Removal of remains also needs a Ministry of Justice licence, which is not published as open data.
  • Worldwide — removed burial grounds in OpenStreetMap. Ground that mappers record as having held a cemetery that is now gone. Community contributed, not an official register, and labelled as such.
  • Worldwide — government open-data portals. Cemetery, burial and excavation datasets found across the portal federations, searched in the languages those portals publish in.
Registers that exist but are not in yet Named so the gaps are legible rather than invisible. Each is real; what is missing is a machine-readable, licence-clear route in.
  • NPS National NAGPRA tables — the Inventories and Unclaimed Lists grids, which carry per-institution counts of individuals still held. The single most valuable gap on this list.
  • Western Australia s.18 consents — the WA equivalent of an AHIP. Spatial reuse terms need checking first.
  • Queensland Cultural Heritage Management Plans register.
  • Spain state Mapa de Fosas — Civil War and dictatorship mass graves, published with coordinates as an act of public memory. The one case where the blurring rule may be the wrong call, since families are meant to be able to find these.
  • Ireland National Monuments excavation licences and excavations.ie.
  • EAMENA — disturbance and looting across the Middle East and North Africa. Account-gated.
  • Colombia UBPD recovery of the disappeared; Mexico CNB clandestine-grave registry.
  • England & Wales Ministry of Justice licences to remove remains (Burial Act 1857 s.25) — issued in volume, released only on request.
  • ICMP — the fullest record of conflict-grave exhumations anywhere; publishes reports, not data.
What the numbers can and cannot tell you
  • No percentage of world coverage is given, because no dataset holds the true global count. Any figure would be invented. The shape of the sample is described instead.
  • A count of individuals appears only where a source states one. Absence of a count means the source was silent, never that the number is small.
  • Coverage is heavily weighted to countries with statutory repatriation and heritage-permit regimes. Sparse regions on this map are sparse in the records, not necessarily in the ground.
